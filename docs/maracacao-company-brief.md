# Maracacao — Company & Content Brief

**A single reference for everything known about the company, its products, and the content the website has to carry.** No design guidance here on purpose: this document describes the business, not how it should look.

Compiled 2026-08-13 from the client's own answers (two questionnaires, 2026-08-07 and 2026-08-12), their online catalogue, and the fifteen wrapper artwork files supplied by their packaging designer. Where a fact came from a measurement rather than from the client, it is marked.

Product names stay in Spanish throughout — they are printed on the packaging and on the labels, and translating them would invent names that don't exist. English glosses are given in parentheses.

---

## 1. What the company is

Maracacao is a Mexican craft chocolate brand based in Mexico City. It makes dark chocolate bars, chocolate drops, and (soon) drinking-chocolate powders, all built on a single idea: a high proportion of real cacao, flavoured only with fruit, chiles, herbs, spices, and natural essences — no artificial flavourings, no unnecessary additives.

Every bar is **70% cacao**, a decision the founders describe as making the chocolate itself the protagonist rather than the sugar. Around that base they build distinctly Mexican flavour combinations: mango with chile, tamarind, chamoy, lime and chile, strawberry with chipotle, alongside more classical ones like cardamom, cinnamon, and sea salt.

The brand launched in **2024**. Its chocolate is produced in **Tabasco**, Mexico's largest cacao-growing region. It sells directly to consumers and to cafés, bakeries, and pastry kitchens.

## 8. What the brand teaches — the chocolate primer

This is the largest body of client-supplied content and the strongest reason the site exists beyond a product list. The client chose four topics and wrote full texts for each, then added a fourteen-question glossary. **All of it is theirs, not invented.**

### 8.1 What 70% cacao means

> A chocolate with 70% cacao retains a significant proportion of the natural components of cacao and generally contains less sugar than chocolates with a low cacao percentage, and lets you better appreciate its natural flavours and aromas, without sugar being the protagonist.
>
> That's why we choose 70% cacao: a percentage that offers intensity, balance, and a greater presence of real cacao. It isn't just about the chocolate being darker: it's about there being more cacao in every bite.

### 8.2 How to taste chocolate — six steps

1. **Look.** Good chocolate should look uniform and, when properly tempered, have a smooth, slightly glossy surface.
2. **Listen.** When you break it, it should give a clean, dry *snap*. In high-cacao chocolates this is especially evident.
3. **Smell.** Before tasting, bring it to your nose. Cacao can reveal fruity, floral, roasted, spiced, earthy, or nutty aromas. Good chocolate doesn't just smell "sweet."
4. **Let it melt.** Place a small piece on the tongue and don't chew it right away. Let the warmth of your mouth slowly release the cocoa butter and its aromas.
5. **Find the flavours.** Notice what appears first and what follows: acidity, sweetness, bitterness, notes of fruit, nuts, coffee, spices. Good chocolate evolves in the mouth.
6. **Watch the finish.** After swallowing, the flavour should linger pleasantly. In a good chocolate the cacao leaves a long, clean, complex sensation, not just sweetness.

Their pull quote for this section:

> Good chocolate isn't eaten in a hurry: you look at it, listen to it, smell it, and let it melt.

### 8.3 Why no additives or flavourings

> We believe good chocolate doesn't need to hide behind a long ingredient list. When the cacao is good quality, its flavour, aroma, and character can speak for themselves.
>
> That's why we prefer simple recipes, with recognisable ingredients and without unnecessary additives, that respect the authentic flavour of cacao.
>
> Fewer artifices. More cacao. More chocolate.

### 8.4 What cacao offers — handle with care

The client supplied a health-benefits text about antioxidants, free radicals, and "oxidative stress pathologies," recommending antioxidant consumption for all ages.

**This text cannot be published as written.** Mexican health-claim regulation (COFEPRIS) restricts attributing preventive or therapeutic properties to a food product. The wording about preventing pathologies is exactly the kind of claim that creates exposure. A softened version keeping only the defensible, compositional part is what belongs on the site:

> Cacao is a natural source of antioxidants, thanks to its flavonoids, and provides minerals such as magnesium, iron, and copper. It contributes to well-being and good spirits, and is a delicious source of energy.

Even that should be reviewed by someone who knows the regulation before it goes live. Flagging it here so it doesn't get quietly reinstated.

### 8.5 Chocolate glossary — the client's fourteen answers

Condensed from their text, this is reference material for a FAQ, an explainer section, or product-page footnotes:

- **What 70% cacao really means.** That percentage corresponds to the ingredients derived from cacao.
- **Why good chocolate doesn't taste only bitter.** It can carry naturally fruity, floral, roasted, nutty, spiced, coffee, or caramel notes.
- **Why cacao origin matters.** Variety, soil, climate, fermentation, and roasting all influence flavour — just as with coffee or wine.
- **Whether darker means more cacao.** Not necessarily. Colour alone doesn't determine quality or percentage. Look at the percentage and, above all, the ingredients.
- **That "crack" when you break a bar.** The sound reflects the chocolate's structure and good tempering.
- **Why it melts in your mouth.** Cocoa butter has the remarkable property of melting near body temperature. That's why good chocolate changes so much when you stop chewing and let it melt.
- **What bloom is.** The whitish film that sometimes appears doesn't necessarily mean the chocolate has gone bad; it usually relates to changes in fat or sugar caused by temperature or humidity.
- **How to store chocolate.** Somewhere cool, dry, out of sunlight, and away from strong odours. The refrigerator is generally not its friend.
- **How to read a chocolate label.** Look at the cacao percentage first — a higher percentage means more cacao-derived ingredients. Check the ingredients: they're listed from most to least. Watch the sugar and added fats. A short, comprehensible list usually says a lot.
- **What cacao is.** The raw material: the fruit and seeds of the cacao tree. Everything else comes from its fermented, dried beans.
- **What cocoa liquor is.** The roasted, ground cacao bean. It naturally contains cacao solids and its butter.
- **What natural cocoa is.** Obtained by removing part of the cocoa butter and grinding the remaining solids. Intense flavour, slightly acidic, with cacao's own notes.
- **What alkalised cocoa is.** Cocoa treated through an alkalisation process. Darker in colour, less acidic, with a softer, rounder, deeper flavour.
- **What a chocolate bar is.** A product made from cacao-derived ingredients and sugar — and, depending on type, others. Its cacao percentage tells us how much of its composition comes from cacao.
- **What powdered chocolate is.** A mixture designed mainly for drinks, generally made with cocoa or cacao powder and sugar; some formulas also include cocoa liquor or other cacao ingredients.

Closing line of the glossary:

> Not everything dark is chocolate, and not all chocolate has much cacao. The label tells the real story.

## 9. Recipes

Four complete recipes, supplied by the client. All four use the drops or the powder, which is deliberate: they demonstrate the products that aren't self-explanatory.

**Hot chocolate with ginger and orange** — *1 cup*
250 ml milk · 30 g ginger-and-orange chocolate drops · a small piece of orange peel
Heat the milk without boiling. Add the drops and stir until melted. Add the orange peel for one minute, then remove. Serve hot.
*Maracacao tip: froth the milk at the end to make it creamier.*

**Cold orange-ginger chocolate** — *1 glass*
30 g ginger-and-orange chocolate drops · 50 ml hot water or milk · 200 ml cold milk · ice · an orange slice
Melt the drops in the hot liquid. Add the cold milk and mix well. Serve over plenty of ice and finish with the orange slice.
*Extra: a small pinch of cinnamon is spectacular with it.*

**Pears with chocolate and cinnamon** — *2 servings*
2 pears · 50 g cinnamon chocolate drops · 2 tbsp water or milk · chopped walnuts or almonds
Cut the pears into wedges and lightly brown them in a pan. Melt the drops with the water or milk and pour over the warm pears. Finish with the nuts.
*Serve with a spoonful of Greek yoghurt or a scoop of vanilla ice cream.*

**Quick chocolate mousse with cardamom** — *4 servings*
80 g cardamom chocolate powder · 200 ml very cold whipping cream · 100 g plain Greek yoghurt · red berries or orange to decorate
Whip the cream until firm. Separately mix the yoghurt with the chocolate powder. Fold in the whipped cream gently and refrigerate for one hour.
*Serve with raspberries, strawberries, or orange segments. Cardamom makes an extremely simple dessert taste far more sophisticated.*

Note that the mousse uses the powder line, which isn't on sale yet.

## 15. Voice and content rules

Standing rules for anyone writing for this brand, established through client feedback rather than assumption:

**Language is Mexican Spanish.** Not neutral Spanish. Mexican vocabulary throughout — *pistaches*, never *pistachos*.

**Never name the character as a monkey.** Client instruction. Describe it or call it "the character."

**No invented business content.** Nothing about the company that the client hasn't stated. This rule exists because earlier drafts invented an origin story, sourcing details, and team descriptions before the real answers arrived. If a fact isn't in this document or in the client's answers, it doesn't go on the page.

**No e-commerce language.** No cart, no checkout, no "buy now." The site informs and routes to the catalogue or to direct contact.

**Concrete headings.** The client rejected wordplay and pun-based titles. Sections are named for what they contain.

**Prices are shown**, as numbers, in Mexican pesos.

**No photographs of people.**

**Health claims stay conservative.** See §8.4.

**Playful but professional.** The client's own framing of the brand's two registers, and both have to be present at once: the packaging illustrations carry the playfulness, the chocolate knowledge carries the professionalism. Neither should cancel the other.
